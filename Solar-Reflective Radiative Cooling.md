Solar-Reflective Radiative Cooling for a Town The objective is not a single paint that reflects every wavelength equally.
It is a set of surface recipes that manage sunlight and heat separately. Incoming solar energy should be reflected before it becomes heat. 
Any heat that is still absorbed should be emitted efficiently as long-wave infrared, especially through the atmosphere’s roughly 8–13 μm transmission window, toward the cold sky. High solar reflectance and high thermal emissivity work together. A surface that absorbs the sun and only later tries to dump the heat is the wrong design.Roofs: very high reflection Roofs are the best place for the most aggressive coating, because they face the sky and do not have to meet driver or pedestrian glare limits. A realistic stack starts with a thin UV-resistant polymer top coat that protects the layers underneath. Beneath that is a solar-reflective pigment layer built around titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and barium sulfate (BaSO₄), both strong white scatterers, with calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) as an abundant filler. A near-infrared reflector—encapsulated aluminum flakes or ceramic infrared pigments—targets energy the eye cannot see. A high-emissivity layer rich in silica (SiO₂) and related ceramics radiates residual heat in the atmospheric window. The result is a polymer–ceramic coating, not simply a thick coat of ordinary white paint.Walls: high reflection, controlled glare Façades use the same principle with a tighter visible-brightness limit. Ceramic infrared-reflective pigments plus TiO₂ in a weather-resistant binder cut solar gain without turning the street into a field of mirrors. Controlled visible reflectance keeps reflected sunlight from flooding neighboring windows and sidewalks, while near-infrared reflectance and thermal emissivity still reduce the heat stored in the wall.Sidewalks: high reflection, comfortable for pedestrians Sidewalks can be lighter than roads. A textured CaCO₃ and TiO₂ coating or light aggregate reflects a useful share of sunlight so the pavement stays cooler underfoot, while the texture diffuses light and avoids mirror-like glare. Comfort and slip resistance set the brightness limit, not the theoretical maximum reflectance.Parking lots: high reflection plus permeability Parking areas can carry a reflective aggregate system using BaSO₄ and TiO₂-type fillers, provided the surface remains porous. Reflectance rejects near-infrared sunlight; permeability lets rain infiltrate instead of running off hot pavement. The lot therefore cools by optics and by avoiding a sealed, heat-storing skin.Roads: moderate visible reflection, strong infrared reflection Roads are deliberately less reflective in the visible band. Tires, braking, oil, water, snowplows, freeze–thaw, and abrasion destroy a roof coating, and a bright white surface creates glare, weakens lane-marking contrast, and can reduce wet skid resistance. The better road is a durable, moderately gray spectrally selective aggregate: visible reflectance stays controlled for driver safety, near-infrared sunlight is strongly reflected, and thermal emissivity remains high so the pavement can radiate heat after sunset. Safety is the constraint the cooling material is not allowed to violate.Parks: shade and evapotranspiration Parks are not solved with paint. Trees intercept sunlight before it reaches the ground and cool the air by evapotranspiration. Shade plus vegetation attacks the heat load at an earlier stage than any surface coating.Windows: solar-control, infrared-selective glazing Glass uses a different tool. Solar-control or low-emissivity coatings reflect near-infrared heat while still admitting visible light, so interiors gain less solar heat without becoming dark.The town as one system Roofs reject most of the sun and radiate leftover heat upward. Walls and sidewalks reflect with glare held in check. Parking lots reflect and drain. Roads look ordinary to drivers while shedding invisible infrared. Trees keep sun off the ground. No single mixture of TiO₂, BaSO₄, and SiO₂ can be assigned a guaranteed town-wide temperature drop; climate, humidity, wind, roof area, pavement area, and the finished spectrum all matter. The design principle is still the same: reflect solar energy where safety allows it, emit thermal infrared everywhere, and let shade do the work coatings cannot.
